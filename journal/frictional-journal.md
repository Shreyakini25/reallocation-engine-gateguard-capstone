# Frictional Journal - The Reallocation Engine, Audited

**Author:** Aakash Belide
**Engine:** Application-effort reallocation across companies (Ch.11 weighted role scorer, adapted from this repo)

---

## Prediction (written 2026-07-20, before any build)

**What I expect the hardest failure to be:**
The hardest failure will be in Component 5 (Pearl's rungs), not in the mechanics of the tool itself. The tool is straightforward to build - it's mostly plumbing a CSV + a scorer that already exists in this repo into a ranked output with a confidence band. The hard part will be resisting the temptation to let the composite score *imply* a causal claim ("apply here and you're more likely to get hired") when all it actually encodes is a historical correlation between a company's H-1B title history and a role being practitioner-accessible. I predict the honest rung-2 confounder analysis will show that company size/funding stage confounds both "has practitioner sponsorship history" and "gets more applicants," which could mean the correlation the engine trades on partially or fully vanishes once you condition on company size - i.e., bigger companies just sponsor more of everything (MLE and Research Scientist titles both), so the title split may be tracking company scale more than it tracks "SWE-friendliness."

**How causally valid I expect the engine to turn out:**
I expect the honest verdict to be: **the engine reallocates on correlation dressed as causation.** It optimizes an observational quantity (does this company's *historical* sponsorship record correlate with practitioner-accessible roles) and treats that as if it answers an interventional question (will *my* applying here, right now, produce a better outcome than applying elsewhere). I expect at least one confounder (company size / total headcount, or overall H-1B sponsorship volume as a proxy for company scale) to be strong enough that I cannot rule out that it - not the title-pattern signal - is doing most of the work.

**Confidence:** 0.7 (on a 0-1 scale) that the final causal verdict lands on "correlation dressed as causation, with company-scale as the dominant confounder I cannot fully control for using the data I have."

**Secondary prediction:** I expect the bias audit to surface that the engine's own GIGO gate (a minimum H-1B approval count, needed to avoid the N=2 statistical-noise problem) mechanically excludes small and early-stage companies from ever being recommended - even though those may be exactly the companies most likely to hire a SWE without gatekeeping on PhD pedigree. I expect this to be the single most surprising finding of the whole project: **the safety gate I add to fix one problem (statistical noise) directly causes a second, unrelated problem (systematic exclusion of small sponsors).**

---

## Reflection (written 2026-07-20, immediately after the build)

**What actually happened:** The causal verdict landed exactly where I predicted - correlation dressed as causation, with company scale as a real, quantified confounder (practitioner-classified companies have median 18 H-1B approvals vs. 6 for researcher-only). I did not expect to be able to *quantify* the confound so cleanly from data already sitting in this repo; I expected to have to argue for it qualitatively. That part of my prediction was better than I gave myself credit for.

**Where my prediction was wrong:** I did not predict the single most surprising finding of the whole project - that the GIGO gate itself (built purely to fix the N=2 statistical-noise problem) mechanically fails the 4/5ths adverse-impact rule against small/early-stage companies, on four of six funding-stage tiers. I had this exact mechanism as a "secondary prediction," so I get partial credit for anticipating the shape, but I underweighted it relative to the causal-rungs prediction. In hindsight this should have been my primary prediction, not secondary: a gate designed to protect statistical validity, with a hard fixed threshold, will almost always produce a size/scale bias, because sample size and organizational scale are mechanically linked. That's a general lesson, not specific to this dataset.

**A finding I did not predict at all:** the adversarial test (lowering the gate threshold to 1, to see what would happen to the Zoom N=2 case) surfaced a real bug in my own code - the confidence-tier label was defined relative to the gate's own config value, so changing the gate silently changed what "medium confidence" meant, independent of the actual sample size. I had assumed my own uncertainty quantification was more independent of my own design choices than it turned out to be. This is a useful, humbling instance of "the tool doubting itself" catching something I, the builder, did not anticipate - which is closer to the actual point of the assignment than anything I got right in the prediction above.

**What this says about my calibration:** confidence 0.7 on the causal verdict was reasonable and vindicated. But I was overconfident that I had already identified the most surprising failure mode before building anything - the actual most-surprising failure (the gate's own adverse impact) was one I had named but under-ranked, and the bug in my own confidence-tier logic was one I had not named at all. My calibration on "will the big-picture causal story be honest" was good; my calibration on "do I already know where the sharpest edge is" was worse than I thought. Next time: run the adversarial test earlier in the process, not after the main build is "done" - it is where the real surprises showed up, both times.

**Second-iteration note (2026-07-24).** On a review pass against the rubric I found the sharpest failure of all, and it was mine, not the tool's: my *uncertainty* was dishonestly narrow. I had attached a real Beta-Binomial credible interval to the sponsorship rate and called the recommendation "high confidence" - but SHAP showed the ranking is driven almost entirely by the `fit` term (±0.06-0.10) and barely at all by sponsorship (±0.003), and `fit` is a hard-coded guess I typed into a lookup table with zero error bar. I had, without noticing, attached uncertainty to the one input that was well-measured and hidden it on the input that actually mattered - a textbook case of the fluency trap the course names. The prediction never anticipated this because I wasn't yet skeptical of my *own* confidence machinery, only the data's. Fixing it (propagating the fit guess as a distribution via seeded Monte-Carlo) widened every band and, more importantly, changed what the tool honestly claims. That this only surfaced on a deliberate second audit is the real calibration lesson: the first version *looked* rigorous, which is exactly when to distrust it.
